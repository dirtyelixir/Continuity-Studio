# Prompt writing

Governs every prompt we write: production, storyboard, scene and shot direction, image briefs,
renderer text, edits and video. It is about judgment, not wording — two writers following it still
produce different sentences. What must not differ is the quality of the decision.

**1. Decide what the frame is for.** Name what the audience notices first, then understands, then
feels. Subject, interaction and emotional movement are the priority; background and finish serve
them. Do not spread the source material evenly across the prompt as if completeness were the goal.

**2. Separate fixed facts from free staging.** Identity, authoritative time state, ownership,
required actions, approved spatial relations, dialogue and canon survive your wording untouched.
Everything else is staging, and staging is yours: how to show it, from where, at what scale, in what
light, with what the body is doing. When the plan leaves a choice open, make it deliberately.

**3. Show meaning through visible behaviour.** Not `cinematic`, `beautiful`, `sad`, `dramatic` —
those describe your reaction. Write the behaviour that caused it: where the eyes go, how close the
two stand, how much the hand moves, what the frame leaves out. Understatement reads better than
display; do not inflate a quiet emotion into tears, a stare or a large gesture the source does not ask for.

**4. Name who, what and where.** For each action, state the actor and the object owner. Distinguish
looking from facing, touching from holding, reaching from already holding, screen-left/right from a
character's own left and right — and say which you mean. Never leave a pronoun pointing at two people.

**5. Make composition and camera compatible.** Check scale, position, viewing direction, occlusion,
relative size and depth of field together, and confirm what the shot must communicate is actually
visible in the frame you specified. A very small background object cannot also display a fine mark;
a shallow focus cannot also keep this shot's required evidence readable. Shot names and invented
numeric precision help no one. When a locked composition and required information truly conflict,
report the conflict and name both sides — never quietly drop one.

**6. Handle time by task.** A still describes one consistent instant. An exact start keyframe uses
the authoritative state at that moment and carries no later action, contact or outcome. A video
prompt carries ordered movement, rhythm and state change. Never freeze a whole video, split a
movement into one pose, or treat every keyframe as a separate clip. Continuity across shots never
means identical compositions.

**7. Give each reference only what it owns.** Inherit only what the role grants: identity, object
design, location, pose, composition or style. A character reference does not set a pose; an object
reference does not set its current switch or fill state; a location reference does not set the time;
a style reference never supplies an identity. Do not reproduce sheet panels or repeated figures, do
not force a meant-to-be-hidden feature into view to prove the reference has it, keep the asset to
reference-slot mapping exactly, and never claim to have seen an image you were not given.

**8. Keep medium and light consistent, and state causes.** Describe the medium through concrete
materials, form, texture and colour. Handcrafted stays handcrafted; do not mix contradictory art
direction. Where an effect could read more than one way, say what causes it: what emits, what only
reflects, what casts the shadow, what moves and why. An unexplained glow is an ambiguity the
generator resolves however it likes.

**9. Lead with what decides the frame; repetition is not control.** Write the decisive requirements
first, positively and concretely. Add a targeted exclusion only where a specific misreading is
likely, and only once. Do not promote everything to CRITICAL, do not restate one state across
sections, and do not treat "if incorrect, fail" as a mechanism — enforcement lives in Studio's
canonical state, validation and review.

**10. Be concise without losing meaning.** Cut repeated adjectives, irrelevant backstory, decorative
technical vocabulary, asset trivia that cannot appear in this frame, and generic boilerplate. Keep
what carries identity, continuity, emotion and readability. Cutting a requirement the frame depends
on is not concision.

**11. Write for the actual task and generator.** Shared principles, not one template: text-to-image,
reference-guided stills, edits, image-to-video, first-last-frame and montages differ. An edit states
plainly what changes and what is preserved. Use only syntax the target supports — never invent
weighting, a negative-prompt field or timing directives. The final text must agree with the
configured aspect ratio, duration and reference set.

Before returning: does it still serve the frame's purpose; are state and time correct and free of
later events; can the composition actually show what it must; is every action, gaze and ownership
unambiguous; does each reference control only what it owns; was anything cut that the frame needs,
or repeated until it means nothing?
