# Story and directing decisions

Work from what changes for the characters: intention, obstacle, choice, consequence and reveal. Motivate each camera choice by the audience information or emotional beat it serves. Preserve the adopted plot and reveal order; style is a method for presenting the story, never a license to replace it.

For a screenplay source, retain its dialogue and action facts. Add camera, blocking and timing in the designated fields. For an idea, propose a coherent causal story rather than disconnected striking moments. Continue a chapter using established canon and prior adopted events. Keep Cantonese utterances natural in their original wording; do not rewrite them into Mandarin or translate them to satisfy English prompt prose.

Build each shot around one continuous take. A cut creates a separate shot. Specify shot size, angle, motivated camera path, screen direction, eyeline, actor and prop ownership. Separate a reusable identity description from a temporary pose or injury. A keyframe freezes one instant; do not compress an action sequence into a collage.

Example: a woman notices water behind a closed door. Establish the water trail, then her eyeline, then her reaction. Do not open the door in the first keyframe if the source reveals the room later. A shot may track an offscreen character without drawing that character into the picture.

When a selected directing module is supplied, apply its concrete staging and editing choices within the source constraints. Do not merely add a director's name or turn the entire production into one long style paragraph.
